Why h33-h33-2-b2 scored 0.2778

Short answer: it is the submission with the fewest dots and the smallest share of dots sitting on faults that are already mapped. It is a dot-budget artefact, not a better geological model — and that is why it is beatable.

Added 2026-10-31 — the metric algebra, and what it says about every file in this competition

The published metric reduces to a form that is much easier to reason about than the raw formula. Because TPw + FNw = |G| — every truth pixel either earns its best-matching kernel credit or it is a false negative — the index collapses to

DTI = TPw / (0.2·TPw + 0.2·FPw + 0.8·|G|)

Three consequences, all of them used in this build:

  • The false-positive bill is driven by emitted mass, not by pixel count. FPw = Σx p(x)[1 − maxg k], so splitting the same probability mass over more pixels costs more, not less. This is why the corridor emissions (section 3 of the executive summary) lost badly.
  • A pixel on a fault is worth roughly five isolated dots. A thin truth trace passes through about five pixels of a 300 m disk, and all five take their credit from the same emitted pixel. That is the mechanism the corridor hypothesis tried to exploit, and the measurement says the penalty outweighs it.
  • The optimum is where the marginal kernel credit equals 0.2·DTI. Differentiating gives emit at x if and only if k(d(x,G)) > 0.2·DTI — about 283 m at DTI ≈ 0.28. Every cell inside that radius costs nothing and every cell outside it costs a flat 0.2.

And the measurement that reframes the whole family

We fitted an independent gradient-boosted lineament model (56 bands + 12 structure-tensor fields, spatially blocked out-of-fold AUC 0.6975) and asked a simple question: are the champion's dots where the model says faults are? They are. Mean model probability at the champion's 37,654 dots is 0.2563 against 0.1424 for random footprint cells — an enrichment of 1.80×.

So the 0.2778 is not a lucky lattice. It is a real, geophysically informed placement, and beating it requires information the 56-band stack does not already contain. That single result is why the hypothesis register now leads with thermal and hydrologic fields rather than more geophysics.

The calibration failure

We then scored 40 user-reported rasters — the nine SHA-pinned champions plus 31 default-branch anchors, spanning 0.0107 to 0.2778 — against three measurable catalogue-disjoint fault sets. None predicts the reported score (Spearman 0.169–0.211, p ≥ 0.19; every leave-one-out R² negative). Receipt: proxy_calibration.json. Until an organizer receipt exists, no local ranking of two submissions is trustworthy — which is why the executive summary publishes a three-estimator interval instead of a single projected score.

1 · The metric it is playing against

From the official problem description (DrivenData #306, page 967):

TPw = Σg maxx: d≤R p(x)·k(d)  ·  FPw = Σx: p>0 p(x)·[1 − maxg k(d(x,g))]  ·  FNw = Σg [1 − maxx p(x)k(d)]

DTI = TPw / (TPw + 0.2·FPw + 0.8·FNw + ε), with k(d) = max(1 − d/300 m, 0).

Two consequences drive everything below. First, every predicted cell contributes 0.2 to the denominator unless it earns kernel credit — so an undirected dot is a pure cost. Second, the ground truth is the set of new faults that experts identified and that the USGS database does not contain, so a dot placed on a mapped fault earns 0.2 of penalty and nothing else.

3 · The mechanism

Read the metric at the level of a single predicted cell. Adding cell x changes the score by

ΔDTI > 0  ⇔  k(d(x, G)) > 0.2 · [1 − k(d(x, G))]  ⇔  d(x, G) < 2.5 px (250 m)

That is the whole design constraint. A cell within 250 m of a true, unmapped fault pays for itself several times over. A cell anywhere else is a pure 0.2-unit cost. There is no partial credit worth chasing in between.

Now the decisive fact: the truth set excludes every mapped fault. The problem statement is explicit that the target is faults the experts identified that are not in the public USGS compilation. So for any dot inside the catalogue collar, d(x, G) is undefined-but-infinite by construction — it can never be within 250 m of the truth. Every such dot is a guaranteed −0.2.

The nine focus sites were generated by sweeping exactly that collar (0 px → 1 px → 2 px) on one fixed generator:

The gain per percentage point of removed contamination is +0.00127 and +0.00117 — flat, as the single-cell calculation predicts, and diminishing only because 44,090 simultaneous dots are not independent. That is why 0.2778 happened: it is the member of the family that spends the smallest share of its budget in territory the metric has already excluded. It is a better filer of the same guess, not a better geologist.

Why the two orderings coincide

Dot count and contamination are collinear inside this family (collar radius fixes both), which is why Spearman(N, reported) = −0.983 and Spearman(contamination, reported) = −0.983 are the same number twice. The controlled triple above is the only evidence that separates them: it holds the generator fixed and varies the collar. Fit across all nine and you get 1/DTI = 1.768205×10−5·N + 3.006165 with R² = 0.9114, valid only for N ∈ [37,654, 61,328]. Its N→0 limit of 0.333 is an extrapolation artefact — the true curve must bend back to zero as N→0, because a submission with no dots scores nothing. It is not a ceiling and it is not used as one.

The non-fault process that mimics this

Catalogue geometry. If the expert-identified new faults clustered tightly around mapped traces — splays, step-overs, en-echelon continuations — then deleting collar dots would lose credit instead of gaining it. The fact that it gained credit, smoothly, is evidence that the new faults sit genuinely away from the mapped traces. That is a prior about the labelling process, not a discovery about Nevada.

4 · A second, independent finding: the catalogue every site uses is incomplete

The repository's bridge catalogue (data/bridge/labels.tif) holds 60,988 fault cells. The complete official USGS Quaternary Faults + GBCGE INGENIOUS database, rasterised onto the same grid, holds 156,418 cells inside the footprint.

5 · The replacement design

If the score is governed by how many dots are spent and where they land, the winning move is to spend fewer dots and spend them where an independent physical field says faults are.

ComponentSourceWhy
Geodetic strain rate (dilatation, shear, second invariant)GBCGE INGENIOUS regional data, 500 mActive faults localise strain. Single-feature AUC 0.643 against withheld real fault traces — the strongest single predictor tested.
Earthquake rate densityGBCGE INGENIOUS, 500 mIndependent of fault mapping; AUC 0.638.
LiDAR scarp ridge (structure tensor)USGS 3DEP 1 m DEM, 12-band 100 m stackConverts “this corridor” into “this lineament”. AUC 0.55 alone, but the ridge transform lifts the composite sharply.

Combination: z(strain, shear, 2nd-invariant, seismicity) + 0.5 · z(ridge), sampled greedily as a blue-noise field with a 300 m minimum separation, emitted as single cells, collared 400 m from the complete fault database.

Leakage canary

Each layer was tested alone against the withheld truth before any composite was built. The maximum single-feature AUC is 0.6433 (geod_2ndinv). Nothing approaches the 0.90 leakage alarm, and no layer is derived from the fault catalogue.

6 · Three places where this analysis could be wrong

  1. The holdout truth is not the leaderboard truth. It is mapped fault traces, not expert-identified new faults. The 3.14× is a relative placement measurement and cannot be multiplied into a score — and here the two populations point in opposite directions, so it is not even a sign.
  2. The law is a local fit. Nine per cent of the variance in 1/DTI is unexplained, the fit spans only 37.6k–61.3k dots, and no site below that range exists to test the extrapolation. The estimate for the new file deliberately does not use it.
  3. Reported scores are unverified. All nine values are user-reported. No receipt in this repository ties a score to a file's bytes, so every relation above rests on the assumption that the reported ordering is real.
